## Probing symmetry breaking orders

Many of the most interesting phases in quantum materials are defined by the symmetries they
break — inversion, time reversal, rotation — rather than by any single-particle observable.
I use nonlinear optics, in particular rotational anisotropy second-harmonic generation
(RA-SHG), to identify these broken symmetries directly and to determine the point group of
the ordered state. Adding a pump pulse turns this into a time-resolved probe, so the order
parameter can be watched as it melts and recovers on femtosecond to picosecond timescales.

## Engineering quantum phases

Driving a material out of equilibrium is not only a way to measure it — it is a way to change
it. Intense light can renormalize exchange interactions, dress the electronic band structure,
and stabilize states that have no equilibrium counterpart. I am interested in using tailored
optical and terahertz drives, including Floquet engineering and resonant phonon excitation,
to switch between competing orders and to create transient phases in strongly correlated and
topological systems.

## Developing new spectroscopy tools

Answering these questions often requires measurements that do not yet exist. A large part of
my work is building the instrumentation: ultrafast optical setups spanning the terahertz to
visible range, and ultrafast diffraction probes such as ultrafast electron diffraction (UED)
and time-resolved X-ray scattering that follow the lattice and charge degrees of freedom
directly. Combining these complementary probes on the same sample lets us disentangle
electronic, magnetic, and structural contributions to the dynamics.
